The short answer

Topical PDRN is a cosmetic ingredient, usually declared on ingredient lists as Sodium DNA or Hydrolyzed DNA. In leave-on serums it is generally considered low-irritation, and most reported discomfort traces back to the rest of the formula — fragrance, alcohol, essential oils, or high-strength actives layered on top — rather than to the DNA fraction itself. PDRN side effects in over-the-counter products are not well characterized in public data, so caution matters most for reactive skin, broken skin, fish allergy, and pregnancy questions.

Key takeaways

  • Topical is not injectable. This article covers over-the-counter cosmetics only. Injectable PDRN products, including the widely discussed salmon-DNA injections, are a clinical procedure and a different risk category entirely.
  • Salmon-derived ingredients are a real allergy question. If you have a diagnosed fish allergy, talk to your clinician before using any salmon-derived cosmetic.
  • The number on the label is often not a PDRN number. Across the 20 PDRN-branded products we checked on August 8, 2026, the biggest advertised figures usually described a blended complex or an exosome count.
  • Price per ml barely moves; cost per mg of disclosed PDRN moves enormously. Among products with a usable disclosed figure, that spread is roughly 280x — and far wider once you include one outlier we name below.
  • Some "PDRN" products are plant extracts. Green tea, ginseng, and seaweed versions exist, and one product in our data set has no DNA-type ingredient in the INCI at all.

What PDRN actually is

PDRN stands for polydeoxyribonucleotide: short chains of DNA fragments, most often sourced from salmon or trout milt. In cosmetic ingredient lists you will rarely see the word "PDRN" itself. What appears instead is Sodium DNA, occasionally Hydrolyzed DNA, and in some products Salmon DNA. That naming gap is the first practical thing to understand, because it is how you check whether a product marketed as PDRN actually contains a DNA-derived ingredient.

The broader ingredient literature on nucleotide fragments has looked at how they behave in skin models and in medical settings, largely around hydration support and barrier-related measures. That work does not transfer automatically to a cosmetic serum: different molecular weight, different concentration, different delivery, different regulatory category. Existing ingredient research explains why formulators are interested. It does not establish that a given bottle will produce a given outcome on your face.

PDRN side effects: what is realistic to expect

Public safety data on topical PDRN in finished cosmetics is thin, and we are not going to pretend otherwise. Here is what can be said responsibly.

Irritation is usually a formula problem, not a PDRN problem

DNA-fragment ingredients are water-soluble, used at low levels, and neither acidic nor exfoliating. Stinging, tightness, or flushing after applying a PDRN ampoule is more plausibly linked to other components. In our data set, one product — SWANICOCO PDRN Hydrating Repair Ampoule — lists Sodium DNA after Fragrance in its own INCI, which tells you fragrance is present in that formula. Fragrance is one of the more common triggers of contact sensitivity in cosmetics generally. Read the full list, not the front of the box.

Fish and shellfish allergy

Salmon-derived ingredients show up throughout this category. Cell Fusion C Salmon PDRN Ampoule Set, for example, declares Sodium DNA plus Salmon Egg Extract. If you have a diagnosed fish allergy, that is a conversation for a board-certified dermatologist or an allergist before you apply anything — not a call to make from a product page.

Layering and over-treatment

PDRN serums are frequently used alongside retinoids, acids, and vitamin C. innisfree Retinol Green Tea PDRN Skin Booster Ampoule is sold as a retinol product, and retinoids have a well-documented adjustment period of dryness and flaking. Pinning that on PDRN would be a misread.

Broken, compromised, or actively inflamed skin

Cosmetics are not designed for use on open or broken skin. If your skin is painful, weeping, blistering, or getting worse rather than better, stop and see a clinician. No serum is the right answer to that question.

Who should approach topical PDRN carefully

  • Anyone with a diagnosed fish or seafood allergy, given how many of these products are salmon-derived.
  • People who react to fragrance. Several products in this category contain it, so check each INCI individually.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding readers. Ingredient-level safety data for topical PDRN in pregnancy is not something we can verify, and some PDRN-branded products also contain retinol, which is routinely avoided in pregnancy. Ask your OB-GYN or dermatologist.
  • Anyone managing an ongoing skin condition with a clinician. Adding an unfamiliar active mid-flare makes it harder for your provider to work out what is going on.
  • Anyone who skips patch testing. Apply a small amount to the inner forearm or behind the ear for a few days before taking it to your face.

None of this means the ingredient is dangerous. It means the risk sits in the finished formula and in your individual history, and neither is knowable from a marketing claim.

The bigger problem: the numbers on the label

If you are researching side effects, you are probably also trying to work out whether a product contains enough PDRN to matter. Here is what our audit of retailer-listed ingredient data found, all figures checked August 8, 2026.

Cosmetic ingredient lists order ingredients above 1% by descending concentration. You cannot tell from the list where that 1% line falls, so a position in the list can never be converted into a concentration or an upper limit. Three patterns stand out.

First, the largest number is usually the least informative. The biggest figure in our data set, 150,000ppm on the PESTLO ampoule, describes a complex — and Sodium DNA still appears near the tail of that product's ingredient list. The brand does not disclose what share of the complex is PDRN.

Second, one brand contradicts itself on a single page. be the skin markets "20,000ppm small molecular PDRN" while its own ingredient list declares "Sodium DNA(2ppm)" — a 10,000x gap between the marketing figure and the declared figure. The brand does not clarify which applies to the finished formula.

Third, the quiet products can be the more transparent ones. Isntree and COSNORI publish no ppm claim at all, yet both place Sodium DNA seventh in their ingredient lists — earlier than several products advertising six-figure ppm numbers. We are not converting that position into a concentration, and neither should anyone else. It is simply a fact about the list, and it sits awkwardly next to the marketing elsewhere in the category.

And some "PDRN" is not DNA at all

The innisfree Retinol Green Tea PDRN Skin Booster Ampoule is branded around "low-molecular Green Tea PDRN™," but the matching INCI entry is Camellia Sinensis Leaf Extract — green tea leaf extract, not polydeoxyribonucleotide or Sodium DNA. VT PDRN Essence 100 markets a ginseng-derived PDRN and lists Panax Ginseng Root Extract near the top of its INCI. Isntree's is "GIM-derived," from seaweed. If you specifically want salmon-derived PDRN — or specifically want to avoid it for allergy reasons — the product name will not tell you. The ingredient list will.

Topical PDRN is not the injectable

REJURAN Turnover Ampoule comes from the brand behind the widely discussed injectable, but it is an over-the-counter topical cosmetic — priced at $52 for 30ml as of August 8, 2026 — and it discloses no PDRN concentration. It also uses Hydrolyzed DNA rather than Sodium DNA. Buying the ampoule is not a home version of an in-clinic procedure, and any question about injectables belongs with a licensed medical provider.

Who topical PDRN may suit

  • People with generally tolerant skin who want a lightweight, water-based hydrating step that layers easily.
  • Readers who want a gentler addition to a routine that already carries retinoids or acids, rather than another exfoliating active.
  • Anyone willing to read an INCI list and choose on disclosure rather than on the biggest number.

Who may prefer to skip it

  • Anyone with a diagnosed fish or seafood allergy, pending clinician advice.
  • Highly reactive or fragrance-sensitive skin, unless the specific formula checks out.
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding readers, until they have discussed it with their own clinician.
  • Anyone hoping a serum will do what a clinical procedure does. It will not, and nothing in this data set supports that expectation.
