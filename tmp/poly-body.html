PDRN and PN (polynucleotides) both come from salmon or trout DNA fragments, both showed up in the same wave of Korean "regenerative" skincare, and both get marketed with near-identical language about bouncy, glassy skin. The stated difference is molecular size: PDRN (polydeoxyribonucleotide) refers to longer DNA fragments, while PN is a broader family term that covers shorter fragments from further-processed DNA. In topical cosmetics, though, that distinction matters far less than concentration and disclosure - and that's where most PDRN vs polynucleotides confusion starts.

PDRN vs Polynucleotides: The Technical Difference

Both come from the same source material - typically salmon or trout milt or roe DNA - and differ mainly in fragment length after enzymatic processing:

  • PDRN (polydeoxyribonucleotide): longer chains of deoxyribonucleotide polymers. This is the term most often referenced in injectable dermatology literature and the term most topical Korean serums print on their labels.
  • PN (polynucleotide): a broader category that can include shorter, more purified fragments. Some brands position PN as a "next generation" or more refined version of PDRN, though brands rarely publish data on the actual molecular differences or how they behave on skin.

Topical Only: This Article Is Not About Injectables

To be clear about scope: everything below concerns over-the-counter topical cosmetics - serums, ampoules and creams you apply at home. It does not cover injectable PDRN or PN treatments administered by a licensed provider in a clinic, which fall under a different regulatory category with a different delivery method and depth of action. Nothing here should be read as guidance on injectable procedures. If you're weighing an in-office treatment, or you have a skin condition, active acne, rosacea or a compromised barrier, talk to a board-certified dermatologist before adding or changing anything in your routine.

Why the PDRN/PN Label Barely Matters at Home

For topical products, whether a brand calls its ingredient "PDRN" or "PN" tells you almost nothing about what's in the bottle. Three things tell you more:

  • Whether the INCI list actually says Sodium DNA (the standard INCI name for PDRN/PN-type ingredients) or something else entirely
  • Whether the brand discloses a concentration in ppm or percentage
  • How that concentration lines up with what you're paying per milliliter

The third point is where the numbers get genuinely strange.

What Disclosed PDRN Concentrations Actually Look Like

Below are retailer-listed INCI details and ppm disclosures for several PDRN-branded products, based on the most recent listings available at the time of writing. Prices are approximate US retail and shift often. The spread is wide enough that "PDRN" on a label means little without a number attached.

  • Anua PDRN Hyaluronic Acid Capsule 100 Serum - 30ml, about $21 ($0.70/ml). The retailer-listed INCI shows Sodium DNA at 100ppm (0.01%); no concentration appears on the label itself. That works out to roughly $7 per mg of Sodium DNA.
  • medicube PDRN Pink Peptide Serum - 30ml, about $22.90 ($0.76/ml). The label states "10,000 ppm," and the INCI lists Sodium DNA at 10,000ppm (1%). That's roughly $0.08 per mg - about 100 times more ingredient per dollar than the Anua serum, at a nearly identical per-ml price.
  • Genabelle PDRN 3% Hyper Boost Ampoule - 40ml, about $29.70 ($0.74/ml). The "3% PDRN" front-label claim is matched by an INCI disclosure of 30,000ppm (3%) - roughly $0.02 per mg, the lowest cost-per-mg in this set.
  • VT PDRN R5 Firming Ampoule - 30ml, about $18 ($0.60/ml). Sodium DNA appears in the INCI, but no concentration is disclosed anywhere, so cost-per-mg can't be calculated.
  • AVCA PDRN Lifting Ampoule - 50ml, about $12.72 ($0.25/ml). The label states "1,000ppm of PDRN" and the INCI matches at 1,000ppm (0.1%) Sodium DNA - roughly $0.25 per mg, a strong value on paper if disclosed concentration is your priority.

The Spread Nobody Advertises

Among the products above that disclose a concentration, cost per milligram of Sodium DNA runs from roughly $0.02 to about $7 - a spread of several hundred times. Price per milliliter, meanwhile, barely moves, clustering in a tight $0.60-$0.76/ml band for most of them. You can pay close to the same amount per bottle for wildly different amounts of the active. Per-ml pricing, the number most shoppers default to, is close to useless for comparing PDRN serums.

When "PDRN" on the Label Isn't What You Think

Two patterns in this category are worth flagging, because they come up more often than you'd expect.

Blended Complexes Wearing a Big Percentage

APLB PDRN Vitamin C Ampoule Serum (40ml, about $9.80, $0.25/ml) advertises "49.1% PDRN VITA CENT" on the label. That number looks enormous next to a 3% claim elsewhere, but it isn't describing pure PDRN content - it's the percentage of a blended complex containing salmon DNA, vitamin C and centella extract together. The INCI lists Salmon DNA, with no standalone concentration disclosed. The biggest number on a label is often measuring the wrong thing.

Plant Extracts Branded as "PDRN"

innisfree Retinol Green Tea PDRN Skin Booster Ampoule (25ml, about $30.50, $1.22/ml) markets a "low-molecular Green Tea PDRNT," but the INCI shows Camellia Sinensis Leaf Extract - not polydeoxyribonucleotide or Sodium DNA. That's a trademarked marketing name borrowing the PDRN association, not the salmon-derived ingredient. If you're shopping specifically for DNA-fragment actives, this one doesn't contain them, whatever the name suggests.

Inconsistent Disclosure, Even Within One Brand

Genabelle discloses 3% (30,000ppm) on its Hyper Boost Ampoule but gives no figure at all on its Genabelle PDRN Vita Toning Ampoule (30ml, about $26.10, $0.87/ml), which simply lists "PDRN (made of Salmon DNA Extracts)" with no ppm anywhere. Same brand, same ingredient story, two very different levels of transparency. That inconsistency is useful information in itself: disclosure is a product-by-product choice, not an industry standard you can assume applies.

So Which Term Should You Trust: PDRN or PN?

Neither term, on its own, is the thing to trust. Whether a Korean serum calls its active "PDRN" or "polynucleotides," the same questions predict what you're actually buying:

  • Does the INCI list Sodium DNA or Salmon DNA specifically - not just a trademarked ingredient name?
  • Is a ppm or percentage disclosed, and does it match the front of the bottle?
  • Does that percentage describe the DNA-fragment ingredient alone, or a blended complex with other actives folded in?
  • What's the cost per mg once you divide price by disclosed concentration, rather than by volume?

User reviews for higher-concentration formulas often mention a temporary plumped or "bouncy" look, but that's subjective feedback, not evidence of a specific outcome - and brand PDRN and PN claims are generally not accompanied by published concentration or study data at the product level. These are cosmetic products intended to moisturize and improve the look of skin; they aren't treatments for any skin condition. Given the current state of Korean beauty labeling, a product disclosing a modest 0.1% Sodium DNA is arguably more transparent than one touting an eye-catching but undefined percentage with no INCI match. Until labeling standards catch up, disclosure - not the PDRN vs. PN label - is the strongest signal shoppers have.

Bottom Line

PDRN and PN differ in fragment length, but on a skincare shelf that distinction is nearly invisible. What isn't invisible, once you check the INCI and ppm figures, is that disclosed concentration and cost per mg vary enormously across products that look nearly identical at checkout. Read past the front-of-label percentage, confirm Sodium DNA or Salmon DNA actually appears in the ingredient list, and treat an undisclosed concentration as missing information rather than proof of anything either way. And if you're targeting a specific skin concern, run your shortlist past a board-certified dermatologist first.
