Below, we check 20 currently available products' label claims against their published ingredient panels, with prices and claims current as of August 8, 2026.

New Korean PDRN August 2026: The State of the Category

PDRN — polydeoxyribonucleotide, typically sourced from salmon or trout milt and declared on INCI panels as Sodium DNA — is still the loudest ingredient story in Korean skincare heading into fall 2026. It is the topical relative of the injectable PDRN used in Rejuran-style clinic procedures. To be clear: every product in this roundup is an over-the-counter cosmetic serum or ampoule. None of them is an injectable, and none is presented here as a treatment for any skin condition.

What has changed since last year's wave of launches isn't the ingredient — it's the marketing math around it. Brands have learned that "ppm" and "%" figures sell, and a growing number of listings now print numbers that sit awkwardly next to the legally required ingredient list. Under cosmetic labeling rules, INCI ingredients above 1% are listed in descending order of concentration; below that threshold, brands can order them freely. So when a brand claims 10% PDRN while Sodium DNA sits at the very end of the panel, the marketing number and the legally required ingredient list are two published facts the brand has not reconciled. The list does not settle it: because the 1% line is invisible, a tail-end position yields neither a concentration nor an upper bound.

One caveat worth stating up front: INCI position is not a measurement of any kind. A high position does not prove a high concentration, a low one does not cap it, and neither says anything about whether a formula will do anything for your skin. If you're weighing an active-heavy serum, or you have reactive or compromised skin, talk to a board-certified dermatologist before adding one to your routine.

Reading the Real Concentrations, Not the Marketing Numbers

Of the 20 products we checked, only a handful pair a specific PDRN percentage with an INCI position that supports it. Here's what holds up.

Products With a Disclosure the INCI Can Support

  • medicube PDRN Pink Peptide Serum — 30 ml, $22.90 ($0.76/ml). The label states 10,000 ppm, and the INCI position is consistent with 10,000 ppm (1%). That works out to roughly $0.08 per mg of disclosed PDRN — among the best value in this data set.
  • Genabelle PDRN 3% Hyper Boost Ampoule — 40 ml, $29.70 ($0.74/ml). Labeled and positioned at 3% (30,000 ppm), or about $0.025 per mg — the lowest cost per mg we found among products with a credible concentration claim.
  • AVCA PDRN Lifting Ampoule — 50 ml, $12.72 ($0.25/ml). The label states 1,000 ppm (0.1%), which the panel supports, for roughly $0.25 per mg. Worth flagging: AVCA's own 2-pack listing for the same product claims 20,000 ppm — a direct contradiction between two of the brand's retail pages.
  • Cell Fusion C Salmon PDRN Ampoule Set — 14 ml (2 ml x 7), $34.20 ($2.44/ml). The label claims 1,000 ppm, supportable at 0.1%, but the single-use vial format pushes cost to about $2.44 per mg — nearly ten times AVCA's figure at the same disclosed concentration.
  • Anua PDRN Hyaluronic Acid Capsule 100 Serum — 30 ml, $21.00 ($0.70/ml). No figure appears on the label, but the INCI is consistent with 100 ppm (0.01%). At roughly $7 per mg of disclosed PDRN, it's one of the most expensive options per unit of active despite a modest sticker price.

Line those five up and the spread is stark: cost per mg of disclosed PDRN runs from about $0.025 to about $7 — a roughly 280x difference — even though price per ml barely moves across the category. A $22 serum and a $30 serum can differ by two orders of magnitude in what you're paying for the active itself.

Products Whose Headline Number Doesn't Match the INCI

This is the more revealing list, because it's where most of the August marketing budget appears to be going.

  • VT PDRN Essence 100 claims "100,000 ppm low-molecular ginseng-derived PDRN," yet Sodium DNA sits at the end of its ingredient list while Panax Ginseng Root Extract sits near the top. The 100,000 ppm figure most plausibly describes the ginseng extract. We can't state a PDRN concentration for this product.
  • PESTLO T3 PDRN Exosome Glow Ampoule advertises "150,000 ppm salmon PDRN complex" — the largest number in this roundup — but "complex" is carrying a lot of weight there, and Sodium DNA still lands in the tail of the panel.
  • SWANICOCO PDRN Hydrating Repair Ampoule claims 20,000 ppm (2%), yet Sodium DNA is listed after Fragrance on the brand's own panel — difficult to reconcile with a 2% claim. It's also the priciest product per ml in our data set at $3.29/ml.
  • be the skin Vitavita Raspberry PDRN Ampoule is the widest gap we found: the same product page markets "20,000 ppm small molecular PDRN" while the printed ingredient list reads "Sodium DNA (2 ppm)" — a 10,000x discrepancy between the marketing copy and the legal disclosure on a single listing.
  • medicube's two Pink Collagen Exosome Shot Serums (2000 and 7500) are named after exosome ppm figures, not PDRN content. The numbers in the product names refer to exosomes; PDRN concentration is undisclosed in both.
  • APLB PDRN Vitamin C Ampoule Serum lists "49.1% PDRN VITA CEN™" at $9.80 for 40 ml — but that 491,000 ppm figure describes a blended complex of salmon PDRN, vitamin C and centella, not PDRN on its own.

Not PDRN at All: The Plant-Extract Problem

One launch deserves its own flag. innisfree Retinol Green Tea PDRN Skin Booster Ampoule markets "low-molecular Green Tea PDRN™," but the corresponding INCI ingredient is Camellia Sinensis Leaf Extract — green tea leaf extract, not polydeoxyribonucleotide or Sodium DNA. Here, "PDRN" is a trademarked marketing term attached to a plant extract rather than the salmon-derived molecule the rest of this roundup covers. Useful to know before assuming every "PDRN" label points to the same ingredient.

The Quiet Launches That May Be Better Bets

Two products in our data disclose no ppm figure at all, yet place Sodium DNA unusually high in their ingredient lists — ahead of several products advertising far bigger numbers:

  • Isntree Gim PDRN Hydro Bouncy Ampoule — 50 ml, $16.24 ($0.32/ml). No concentration claim, but Sodium DNA appears 7th on the INCI.
  • COSNORI PDRN Revitalizing Ampoule — 50 ml, $23.00 ($0.46/ml). Also no ppm claim, also 7th on the panel.

Neither brand gives grounds to state a specific concentration, so we won't. But ingredient-list position is a more reliable signal than most of the headline percentages on this list — with the caveat that position alone still can't tell you the exact amount.

Korea Launch vs US Availability

Most of the formulas above circulated in the Korean market before appearing on US-facing retailer sites and marketplaces, and ppm disclosures usually originate on Korean product pages, then get translated - sometimes incompletely - for US listings. When a US listing is missing a figure that the Korean page shows, that's often a localization gap worth raising with the retailer. It isn't proof the ingredient is absent, but it also isn't something to assume in the brand's favor.

What About Rejuran's Topical Line?

REJURAN Turnover Ampoule ($52 for 30 ml, $1.73/ml) comes from the brand best known for injectable PDRN procedures performed in clinics. This is a separate product category: an over-the-counter topical cosmetic, and the only thing this article is discussing. Nothing about the injectable line's positioning transfers to the bottle. The ampoule labels its ingredient "c-PDRN®" and uses Hydrolyzed DNA on the INCI rather than Sodium DNA, and the brand discloses no concentration figure. The name recognition carries a clear price premium without a matching disclosure advantage.

Bottom Line for August 2026

If you want a serum whose PDRN concentration can actually be checked against the ingredient list, Genabelle's 3% Hyper Boost Ampoule and medicube's Pink Peptide Serum are the two strongest candidates in this batch, on both disclosure and cost per mg. Anything fronted by a five- or six-figure ppm number is worth a second look at where Sodium DNA actually sits on the panel before you take the claim at face value. And if the label says "PDRN" while the INCI says green tea or ginseng, you're buying a different ingredient than you think.

Disclosure quality is not the same thing as performance, and none of these products is intended to diagnose, treat or prevent any skin condition. If you're managing acne, rosacea, eczema or post-procedure skin, check with a board-certified dermatologist before layering in a new active.

Prices and claims are current as of August 8, 2026 and can change with sales or reformulation, so it's worth rechecking the retailer's own ingredient panel before you buy.
