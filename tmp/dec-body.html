Key takeaways

  • 10,000ppm = 1%. That conversion is the only arithmetic you need.
  • Ingredient lists run in descending order only above about 1%, and the 1% line is invisible. A tail-end Sodium DNA entry is a question worth asking, not a number.
  • INCI position is not a measurement of any kind. It can expose a claim that does not add up; it cannot establish a percentage or an upper bound.
  • A missing number is not automatically worse than a contradicted one.

On most Korean PDRN products, the large ppm or percentage number on the front of the bottle is not a reliable measure of PDRN concentration. It often describes a blended complex, an exosome count, or a figure that cannot be reconciled with the brand's own ingredient list.

This is the method for checking one yourself. It takes about five seconds per product on a retailer page. For the full audit of what 20 products disclose, see our guide to what PDRN actually is.

What "PDRN concentration" actually means

PDRN stands for polydeoxyribonucleotide, a fragmented DNA compound typically sourced from salmon or trout milt. On a cosmetic label it appears in the ingredient list as Sodium DNA — its INCI name — or, less often, Hydrolyzed DNA.

Concentration is expressed in ppm or percent, where 10,000ppm equals 1%. You would expect a higher number to mean more active ingredient. In practice, the figure printed in bold on the box and the amount of PDRN in the formula are frequently two different things.

The rule that makes label-reading possible

Under standard cosmetic labelling convention, ingredients are listed in descending order of concentration — but only reliably down to the 1% mark. Below that threshold, brands may list ingredients in more or less any order.

How to use it: if a brand advertises 100,000ppm (10%) PDRN but Sodium DNA appears near the very end of its own ingredient list, those are two published facts that the brand has not reconciled. You can check that in seconds on the retailer's ingredient panel, and it is the question to put to the brand.
The limit of this method: because ingredients below roughly 1% may be listed in any order, you cannot tell where that line falls. So a position yields neither a concentration nor an upper bound. It flags a claim worth questioning — nothing more, and we never convert a position into a number.

Five patterns to watch for

1. The number describes something other than PDRN

Plenty of products print a large ppm figure that measures a blended complex, an exosome count or a proprietary-named ingredient rather than isolated PDRN.

What the brand says
APLB PDRN Vitamin C Ampoule Serum advertises "49.1% PDRN VITA CEN™" — 491,000ppm, the largest figure we encountered.
What the disclosure says
The brand attributes that figure to a blended complex of salmon PDRN, vitamin C and centella.
What follows
The number describes the blend. It cannot be read as the concentration of any single component, including PDRN.
What is still unknown
The composition of the complex is not published, so no PDRN figure can be derived from it at all.

Transparency: Complex Disclosure rating, not an efficacy rating

The same pattern shows up elsewhere. PESTLO T3 PDRN Exosome Glow Ampoule claims 150,000ppm, which the brand labels a "salmon PDRN complex" figure, while Sodium DNA still sits at the end of the INCI list. medicube PDRN Pink Collagen Exosome Shot Serum 2000 and its sibling, the Exosome Shot Serum 7500, put exosome ppm figures — 2,000 and 7,500 — directly in the product names, while PDRN content in both is undisclosed.

2. The marketing number contradicts the brand's own ingredient list

This is the clearest, most verifiable mismatch in the category.

What the brand says
be the skin Vitavita Raspberry PDRN Ampoule markets "20,000ppm small molecular PDRN."
What the disclosure says
The brand's own printed ingredient list declares "Sodium DNA (2ppm)."
What follows
Two brand-published figures for the same product differ by roughly 10,000x. Both cannot be describing the same thing.
What is still unknown
Which figure is intended, and what the 20,000ppm refers to, cannot be established from what is published.

Transparency: Conflicting Disclosure rating, not an efficacy rating

Two more in the same shape. VT PDRN Essence 100 claims "100,000ppm low-molecular ginseng-derived PDRN" (10%), yet Sodium DNA is the last ingredient listed while Panax Ginseng Root Extract sits near the top — the headline number most plausibly refers to the ginseng extract. SWANICOCO PDRN Hydrating Repair Ampoule claims "20,000ppm PDRN extracted from Salmon" (2%), but Sodium DNA appears after Fragrance on the list, which is difficult to square with a 2% claim. It is also the highest price per millilitre in our set, at about $3.29/ml.

3. "PDRN" branding attached to a non-PDRN ingredient

Not everything labelled PDRN is DNA-derived. innisfree Retinol Green Tea PDRN Skin Booster Ampoule markets "low-molecular Green Tea PDRN™," but the corresponding INCI ingredient is Camellia Sinensis Leaf Extract — a plant extract, not polydeoxyribonucleotide. Several other ampoules advertise "ginseng-derived" or "seaweed/GIM-derived" PDRN, using the term as branding rather than an ingredient category.

4. Disclosure that holds up to a cross-check

A handful of products state a figure and place Sodium DNA where that figure is plausible. Cost-per-mg numbers are our own calculations from list price, volume and the stated concentration — not brand-published figures.

  • Genabelle PDRN 3% Hyper Boost Ampoule — 3% (30,000ppm), consistent with its INCI placement, at roughly $0.025 per mg.
  • medicube PDRN Pink Peptide Serum — 10,000ppm (1%), roughly $0.076 per mg.
  • Cell Fusion C Salmon PDRN Ampoule Set — 1,000ppm (0.1%), matching its mid-list INCI placement, roughly $2.44 per mg.
  • Anua PDRN Hyaluronic Acid Capsule 100 Serum — 100ppm (0.01%), consistent with its INCI placement, roughly $7 per mg.
  • AVCA PDRN Lifting Ampoule — lists 1,000ppm (0.1%), consistent with its INCI, but the brand's 2-pack listing of the same product states 20,000ppm. Treat it as unresolved.

5. Silence can be more honest than a big number

Two products make no ppm claim at all, yet place Sodium DNA higher in the ingredient list than several products advertising six-figure ppm numbers: Isntree Gim PDRN Hydro Bouncy Ampoule and COSNORI PDRN Revitalizing Ampoule, both with Sodium DNA 7th on the INCI list.

Neither position converts into a percentage without more information, so we are not going to invent one. Still, placement that high suggests these formulas may hold more PDRN than some louder alternatives.

Why price per millilitre tells you almost nothing

Across the listings we checked, price per millilitre for PDRN ampoules and serums clusters fairly tightly — mostly between about $0.25 and $1.25/ml — regardless of the concentration advertised.

What swings wildly is cost per milligram of PDRN, because that depends on concentration — and concentration claims run from cross-checkable to flatly contradicted. Among products with disclosed, INCI-consistent figures, cost per mg spans roughly 280x, from about $0.025 to $7.

One product's own ingredient list declares just 2ppm against a 20,000ppm marketing claim. Including it would push the spread into the hundreds of thousands, but that reflects a labelling contradiction rather than a real market range, so we leave it out of the comparison.

A practical checklist before you buy

  1. Pull up the full INCI list on the retailer page instead of trusting the front-of-package number.
  2. Find Sodium DNA or Hydrolyzed DNA and note where it falls. Near the top or middle tells you more than near the end.
  3. If the label claims more than roughly 1% (10,000ppm+) and Sodium DNA sits near the bottom of the list, treat the headline number as unconfirmed — the brand has published two things that do not obviously fit.
  4. Watch for "complex," "exosome" or a trademarked name sitting next to the number — those usually describe a blend, not isolated PDRN.
  5. Confirm the ingredient is actually DNA-derived and not a plant extract wearing a "PDRN" name.
  6. Remember that no disclosed number is not necessarily worse than a big, contradicted one.

The bottom line

PDRN concentration claims across Korean skincare are inconsistent enough that the number on the bottle reads as a marketing starting point rather than a fact. Cross-checking it against the brand's own ingredient list takes seconds on a retailer page and routinely exposes the gap between what is advertised and what the formula's own disclosure allows.

Until the category standardises disclosure, the ingredient list is the most reliable thing you have — and as a few of these examples show, even it sometimes disagrees with itself.
